# Scratch Game Mechanics: Plan, Build, and Improve a Game

Plan and build a small Scratch game over seven lessons. Choose a theme and
game idea, then develop it one mechanic at a time. Your game should let a
player take an action, face a challenge, track progress, and reach a clear
ending.

You have already used variables and selection in your quiz. This project
reuses those ideas in a different context and introduces game controls,
interactions, testing, and improvement.

## What you will practise

- planning a game and making a simple prototype;
- using a loop and keyboard controls to move a sprite;
- connecting scripts so sprites respond to one another;
- using variables and conditions as part of a game mechanic;
- creating a reusable custom block;
- testing, debugging, and improving a project.

## Choose a game idea

Choose one idea, or create your own with your teacher's approval:

- **Collector:** move around and collect items to reach a target score.
- **Dodger:** move around and avoid a moving hazard for as long as possible.
- **Maze escape:** find a route through a small maze and reach the exit.
- **Patrol challenge:** avoid an enemy moving between two points.
- **Target catcher:** move to catch targets that appear around the screen.

Not sure? Choose **Collector**. Start with one player sprite, three items,
one hazard, and a target score of three.

## Your minimum playable game

By the end, your game should have:

1. a player action or control;
2. an object, target, or challenge to respond to;
3. a variable or other clear way to show progress;
4. a condition that leads to a win or game-over ending; and
5. a way to restart and try again.

Build the core game before adding extras. More sprites and effects do not
automatically make a better game: make sure the controls and rules work
first.

## Keep a project log

At the end of each lesson, record a short entry in your project log. This
can be a printed sheet kept with your work or a page in your computing
book. It is your reliable record, even if you cannot upload anything that
day:

1. **I added:** the feature you built today.
2. **Evidence:** a screenshot, a quick sketch of the blocks or game screen,
   or a short description of the feature.
3. **I tested:** what you tried and what you expected.
4. **The result:** what happened.
5. **Next:** one specific thing you will work on next lesson.

Save the Scratch project using your teacher's instructions after every
lesson. Keep the same project so that each week's work builds on the last.
If you have a straightforward way to upload evidence, your teacher may ask
you to submit a few milestone items rather than something every lesson:

- **Lesson 1:** a screenshot of your plan or first prototype;
- **Lesson 5:** a screenshot showing your working game and its win or
  game-over rule;
- **Lesson 6:** your test result and the change you made; and
- **Lesson 7:** your final Scratch project link or project file, if your
  teacher requests it.

Use the submission method your teacher gives you. Do not share personal
account details. If uploading is difficult, keep the evidence in your
computing book and be ready to demonstrate your game in class.

## Optional paper-based homework

The game is built and tested in lessons. Homework can be completed on
paper; it does not require Scratch, a device, or an upload. Use one or two
of these short reflections if they suit your class:

- **After Lesson 3:** Draw or describe how touching an item changes the
  score. Explain why the score is set to zero when the game starts.
- **After Lesson 6:** Describe one problem your playtester found, the
  change you made, and what happened when you repeated the test.
- **Before Lesson 7:** Write a short introduction to your game and one
  mechanic you will explain during the showcase.

Pupils who cannot complete homework can do the reflection in class or
explain it during the showcase. The project log and saved Scratch project
remain the main evidence.
